So first thing is pretty amazing. You see that Daniel, named Belteshazzar—because remember, Nebuchadnezzar is the narrator here—has genuine concern for him. “My lord, may the dream be for those who hate you, its interpretation for your enemies.” I really wish this weren't about you.

And yet, there's an interesting thing. I think Nebuchadnezzar already roughly understands what the dream means, but he wants confirmation. And you wonder why all the other wise men could not declare the interpretation to him. It's quite possible that the other wise men felt it was clear as day exactly what the dream meant, but they were afraid of losing their lives if they actually told the king, “This is what it means.” You don't want to be the bearer of bad news to the king, especially Nebuchadnezzar, especially the one who will tear you limb from limb and make your house a rubbish heap.

Daniel has real courage as he is telling Nebuchadnezzar what the dream means. And of course, there is revelation from the Lord because, spelling out what exactly it is, starting in verse 24, he says, “This is the interpretation, O king.” And he does spell it out beyond what Nebuchadnezzar has at least been able to say about his dream so far.

In Daniel's interpretation, he almost repeats—not quite verbatim, but he's repeating a lot of the dream again. Throughout, he's actually highlighting some of the significance of different elements of the dream. For example, in verse 20, “The tree you saw, which grew and became strong, so its top reached to heaven.” What's the significance of it? It's significant because he's saying that the empire of Babylon is prominent. It's central. Everyone can see it because it's visible to the end of the whole earth. That's what Daniel says.

Several other characteristics: it's tall, it's central, and it's fruitful. Daniel mentions that as well. And then his interpretation: “It's you.” Even though Nebuchadnezzar likely suspected that, he spells it out clearly.

And then continues on and gives the meaning of the scary part of the dream. The second part, not just of the vision of the tree and how tall it was and how central and all the branches and the birds and the beasts and the fruit. But what is the meaning of it being chopped down?

Daniel picks up on what Nebuchadnezzar said at the end, which is the point is “until you know” Daniel is addressing you, Nebuchadnezzar. Until you personally know “that the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom He will.” It is saying all authority that anyone has was actually given from above.

That's actually what the centurion says when he's asking Jesus to heal his servant. He asks Jesus, and Jesus said, basically, “Shall I come with you?” And he says, “No, just say the word and my servant will be healed. For I'm also a man under authority. And when I say to this one, ‘Come,’ he comes, and that one, ‘Go,’ he goes.”

Jesus marvels at his faith because he understands that all the authority that the centurion (the commander of 100) has is given from a higher power. Nebuchadnezzar feels that his authority originates from himself. This entire judgment, this entire sentence, is to the end that he would know that he's actually receiving authority from the Most High, from the Lord.

And that note of grace of the leaving of the stump.“As it was commanded to leave the stump of the roots of the tree, your kingdom shall be confirmed for you.”

There's this gracious protection and almost like preservation of the empire—which only God could do, because if a powerful ruler is disabled, there are many who are ready to step up and take it over and end his life. This is remarkable protection from above. The Lord still has mighty purposes for Nebuchadnezzar and is giving him the nth chance.

Note the final thing that Daniel says. He hears this dream and he bears the bad news, and he could have just left it there, but he doesn't. Verse 27: **“Therefore, O King, let my counsel be acceptable to you. Break off your sins by practicing righteousness and your iniquities by showing mercy to the oppressed, that there may perhaps be a lengthening of your prosperity.”**

Daniel, even at this point, out of his compassion for Nebuchadnezzar, as well as his wanting to see the Lord's peace and real mercy going forth, is giving him this chance to say, “If you repent, if you turn back now, perhaps the calamity and the dream that you had can be averted.”

And how do we speak to those in positions of authority? Are we just out there saying, “You've got it all wrong”? For Daniel, he has come to this place where he gives him the bottom line of: this is what the Lord desires for you to know: that the authority comes from Him. And then this is what the Lord desires for you to do: which is practice righteousness and show mercy to the oppressed.

And it's really amazing because Nebuchadnezzar is the one who's responsible for leveling Jerusalem, leveling the temple, exiling Daniel and his friends and his people for the rest of their lives. But Daniel has joined the Lord in His compassionate heart to reach all people. As we have noted in past weeks, Jeremiah writes, Jeremiah 29, and says, “When you are exiled, pray for the welfare of the place where you are taken and work for its welfare, because in its welfare is your welfare.”

He has really taken the Lord's heart on instead of being bitter at this man who ended his entire life as he knew it. We never hear of Daniel's parents again. They may have died in that whole time. He indeed spends the rest of his life in exile, but just as devoted to the Lord. And then, because of that, being used powerfully at the top echelons of power, not only during the reign of Babylon, but then the following one of the Median Persian Empire. With Darius and Cyrus, we hear of him even through those reigns.

He does not change in his orientation to serve the Lord faithfully wherever he is. The Lord uses that powerfully. Here he is displaying the Lord's heart for this man who is, after all, a man. He is the most powerful king, but he's a man in need. He's afraid, even though he's the great king, because a declaration has come from above him that he will be chopped down, that all he's accomplished will be finished.

Daniel could have been rejoicing in that: “Boy, you're getting what you deserve.” If he had developed a bitter heart and a victim mentality, he would have been rejoicing, because that's all about me and what I'm experiencing and did I like it or not. But here he has taken the Lord's heart, which is for this man as for any other man.

He is urging him at risk of his life. You don't normally say to the most powerful man in that part of the world, “You need to repent.” It doesn't tend to make friends. Kings like to silence that kind of voice. Prophets get killed. But here it's so clear that he really genuinely cares personally for Nebuchadnezzar and for what happens to him. And he's saying, “Let my counsel be acceptable to you.” Still, “O King,” he honors the authority, the secular authority that is established by God Himself.

He's honoring that no matter how it's been exercised by that person. But he's saying, “There is yet hope for you.” This is not fatalism. This is not cast in concrete. That there is an opportunity for you to avert this disastrous judgment. Repent, change your mind, change your direction. And these are what would signify that you really have changed, is not only acknowledging that the Most High is the One who establishes rule, human rule, from including to the lowliest. You wonder if Nebuchadnezzar remembered that when he was at his lowliest.

But what would be the sign of it? “Practice righteousness.” Practice the character of God. And he certainly had seen that in Daniel. We only hear about a few encounters. Daniel, he's been made the ruler of Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar. He is in the court. The king would have gotten to know him well and clearly trusts him. There's a personal relationship of trust, and he knows clearly that Daniel really cares about him. And that trust relationship has been established over time because not just fear, but honor of authority and real personal care for this man.

“Practice righteousness” and “show mercy to the oppressed.” “Break off your iniquities.” And these are what they are. And he's specific that “perhaps there'd be a lengthening of your prosperity.” He's saying, “There's a chance. This isn't cast in concrete.”

And I don't know how they viewed the Babylonian gods. I know with the Greek pantheon, there was a huge sense of fatalism that once it was cast by the gods, you had no choice, no free will. It was just going to happen. And then you saw how it played out in all of the Greek tragedies. But here, the true God looks for repentance, for a change of heart, and is right there to welcome and move the person forward into all that they were created to be. And that's what Daniel is urging this king, this powerful man. And he really cares personally.
